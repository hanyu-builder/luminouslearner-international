The Optical Physics

Total Magnification Formula Magtotal = Eyepiece Lens (10x) × Objective Lens (4x, 10x, 40x, or 100x)

A compound microscope uses two sets of lenses to multiply the image size. The ocular lens (eyepiece) is typically fixed at 10x. You multiply this by the selected objective lens on the nosepiece (e.g., 40x) to find your total magnification (400x).

Core Scientific Concepts

The Parfocal Rule

High-quality microscopes are "parfocal," meaning if a specimen is in focus on low power, it will remain mostly in focus when you switch to high power. If you completely lose your specimen, you must drop back to 10x to find it again!

Depth of Field (DoF)

As magnification increases, the depth of field (the 3D vertical thickness of the specimen that is sharply in focus at one time) becomes incredibly shallow. This is why you must constantly tweak the fine focus knob at high power.

Refraction & Immersion Oil

Light bends (refracts) when moving from a glass slide into the air, missing the tiny 100x lens. Immersion oil has the exact same refractive index as glass, channeling the light beams straight up into the lens for a crisp, high-resolution image.

Comparing Focus Knobs

Coarse Focus

  • Moves the mechanical stage up and down in large, visible increments.
  • Used strictly for finding the initial image on 4x or 10x lenses.
  • Highly dangerous to use on high power; it can easily crush the glass slide.

Fine Focus

  • Moves the stage in microscopic, nearly invisible increments.
  • Used to achieve a tack-sharp, highly resolved image.
  • The ONLY focus knob you are permitted to touch when using 40x or 100x lenses.

Exam Corner: Tips and Tricks

⚠️ Exam Trap: The "High Power Coarse Focus" Trap

A classic practical exam trap asks what you should do if an image is slightly blurry while looking through the 40x objective. Many students guess "adjust the coarse focus." This is an instant fail! Turning the coarse knob on high power will smash the objective lens through the glass slide. Always answer: "Adjust the fine focus knob."

Test Your Knowledge

❓ Q: If you are looking through a 10x eyepiece and using the highest power dry objective (40x), what is your total magnification?
Answer: 400x.

Total magnification is the product of the ocular lens and the objective lens. Therefore, 10 multiplied by 40 equals 400.

❓ Q: Why does the simulator screen look washed out or completely white sometimes?
Answer: The light intensity is too high.

You must lower the illumination or close the iris diaphragm to create contrast. Lower magnifications require much less light than high magnifications.

❓ Q: What happens to the field of view as you increase magnification?
Answer: It decreases.

The physical area you can see shrinks drastically as you zoom in. This is why you must center the specimen perfectly on low power before switching to high power.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why can't I use the coarse focus knob on 40x or 100x?
The high-power lenses are physically much longer and sit extremely close to the glass slide. The coarse focus moves the stage too fast, creating a very high risk of driving the lens straight through the glass slide, which can shatter the slide and permanently scratch the expensive lens.
What is the purpose of immersion oil?
At 1000x total magnification, the viewing aperture of the objective lens is incredibly tiny. Without oil, light bends (refracts) as it leaves the glass slide and enters the air, missing the lens completely and leaving a blurry image. Oil bridges the gap, trapping the light and directing it into the lens.
Why did my specimen disappear when I switched to 40x?
It likely wasn't perfectly centered when you were looking at it on 10x. Because the field of view shrinks drastically at 40x, a specimen that is slightly off-center on low power will end up completely outside your viewing area on high power. Switch back to 10x, center it, and try again!
What are the typical objective lens powers on a compound microscope?
Most standard educational microscopes are equipped with four objective lenses on the revolving nosepiece: 4x (Scanning lens), 10x (Low Power), 40x (High Power Dry), and 100x (Oil Immersion lens).
How do I properly carry a microscope in the real lab?
Always use two hands: one hand firmly grasping the sturdy arm (the back curved portion) of the microscope, and the other hand placed flat underneath the heavy base to provide total support.

🔍 Explore Related Concepts

  • ▹ Resolution: The ability of a microscope's lenses to distinguish two incredibly close points as separate, distinct objects rather than a single blurry blob.
  • ▹ Field of View (FOV): The maximum illuminated area visible when looking through the eyepiece, which mathematically shrinks as magnification increases.
  • ▹ Refractive Index: A physics measurement indicating how much light bends when passing through a medium, crucial for understanding why oil immersion is necessary.
  • ▹ Condenser Lens: A lens system located immediately under the mechanical stage that concentrates and focuses light from the illuminator directly onto the specimen.
  • ▹ Electron Microscopes: Highly advanced laboratory tools that use beams of electrons instead of light to achieve millions of times of magnification for viewing viruses.
